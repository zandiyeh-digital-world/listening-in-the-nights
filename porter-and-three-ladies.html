Night 32 · Inside the house

The condition

The shopper argues that the porter should be allowed to stay. The women agree to host him on condition that he refrain from asking them to explain their actions. They direct him to the inscription at the entrance, and he gives his word. The condition is repeated when the dervishes arrive.

Who speaks and who listens?
The shopper makes a case for keeping the guest. The women settle the terms, and the porter hears and accepts them.
What does the guest know?
He knows the terms before he agrees to stay. The women have made room for his company while retaining a say over what he can ask them to disclose.
Source note

Haddawy, The Story of the Porter and the Three Ladies, Night 32, the porter’s pledge and the arrival of the dervishes. Scene paraphrased.

Night 36 · The pledge is broken

The guests’ question

The guests have seen two black dogs beaten and wept over, and marks of whipping on one of the women. Ja’far reminds the caliph of the pledge. The caliph presses for an explanation, and the group chooses the porter to ask. The lady calls for armed men to restrain the guests. She questions them and makes telling how they came to the house a condition of release.

Who speaks and who listens?
The porter speaks for the group. Ja’far stays silent when the lady asks whether the others endorse the question. The guests soon find themselves answering questions of their own.
What does the lady infer?
She reads the men’s confidence as a sign that they may hold rank. Their boldness prompts the inference. Their identities still have to be learned through questioning.
What changes when she sets the terms?
The lady uses the threat of violence to demand accounts. Her control of the exchange gives her access to information and puts pressure on the guests to disclose it.
Source note

Haddawy, The Story of the Porter and the Three Ladies, Night 36, the porter’s question, Ja’far’s silence and the lady’s terms for release. Scene paraphrased.

Nights 62–69 · At the caliph’s court

The women’s accounts

The caliph has the women brought to court. Ja’far reveals the caliph’s identity and relays his questions. Two women give separate accounts. The explanations withheld in the house are now told before an audience with the authority to demand them.

Hear the mistress of the house

She identifies the dogs as her sisters and recounts the events that led to their transformation. In her account of a voyage, she describes her wealth and her responsibilities as head of a household when she proposes that a young man travel with her to Baghdad.

What her telling adds. Her account shows her reasoning about money, travel and obligations to her sisters. The guests’ view of the household had revealed little of those decisions.

Haddawy, The Tale of the First Lady, the Mistress of the House, beginning in Night 63. Account paraphrased.

Hear the woman who was flogged

She explains how an injury in the market led to her husband’s questioning and violence. When he threatens to punish groups of workers based on her explanations, she objects that innocent people would die.

What her telling adds. Her account preserves her judgment about whom a powerful listener might blame. It also shows how little room she has to explain herself safely while he presses for an answer.

Haddawy, The Tale of the Second Lady, the Flogged One, especially Night 68. Account paraphrased.

Whose account is missing?
The shopper helps decide who stays and cares for her sister in the house. The court sequence gives the other two women extended accounts of their own. The shopper’s agency is visible chiefly through what she says and does within their stories.

How does moving from the house to the court change what each woman can decide about telling her story?

Source note

Haddawy, the court summons before Night 63, the two ladies’ tales and the concluding explanations in Night 69. The three reading stages select episodes from a longer sequence that also contains the guests’ stories.

Sources and interpretation

The scenes follow Husain Haddawy’s translation of The Arabian Nights, New Deluxe Edition, based on Muhsin Mahdi’s edition. The chapter brings selected episodes from The Story of the Porter and the Three Ladies together with the two ladies’ accounts. Consulted edition

The reading draws on Zahra Zandiyeh’s Teaching through Tales, MA research paper, University of Guelph, Fall 2025, especially its discussion of speech, silence and female authority in the Baghdad household.
